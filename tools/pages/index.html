How CryoPaint compares

How hot each kind of roof gets on a 100°F summer day, in full sun with little wind, measured on top of the roof mid-afternoon. Each bar runs from the coolest to the hottest published reading for that material.

Conditions and Sources

Test conditions. A 100°F day, clear sky, light wind, mid-afternoon, measured on the top surface of the roof in direct sun. No indoor, attic, under-deck or shaded readings.

Method. Peak surface temperatures from published field measurements, adjusted to a common 100°F day using the air temperature each study recorded. Bare metal is quoted directly from the EPA. Every figure is cited below.

Dark shingle roof, 167–179°F

  • 171.3°F with air at 104°F. Black EPDM membrane, Long Island City NY, July 2011. Gaffin et al., Environmental Research Letters 7:014029 (2012), Table 3.
  • 164.1°F with daily max air 85 to 95°F. Asphalt shingle panel, August 2000. Kriner, Miller & Desjarlais, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Buildings VIII, Table 2.
  • Corroboration: EPA, Reducing Urban Heat Islands, Ch. 4 (draft): black asphalt roofs reach 165 to 185°F at summer midday.

Bare metal roof, 150–165°F

  • Quoted directly from EPA, Reducing Urban Heat Islands, Ch. 4 (draft).
  • Also measured: ORNL Buildings VIII, Table A-1: unpainted galvanized steel peaked at 156.0°F, 55% Al-Zn at 147.8°F (no air temperature published, so not used to set the bar).

White paint on metal, 128–138°F

  • 123.0°F with daily max air 85 to 95°F. White PVDF painted galvanized steel, weathered reflectance 0.610. ORNL Buildings VIII, Table 2.
  • Reflectance basis: 0.55 to 0.70 for white painted metal. A Practical Guide to Cool Roofs and Cool Pavements, Global Cool Cities Alliance, 2012.

Elastomeric cool roof coating, 115–140°F

  • 120°F with outdoor air peaking just under 105°F. Davis CA, July 1997. Akbari, Gartland & Konopacki, LBNL, ACEEE Summer Study 1998.
  • 135°F with outdoor air about 95°F. San Jose CA, August 1997. Same study.
  • 127.6°F with air at 104°F. White acrylic elastomeric, MoMA Queens NY, July 2011. Gaffin et al. (2012).

White TPO or PVC roof, 109–124°F

  • 106.1°F with max outdoor air about 97°F. New white thermoplastic membrane, August 1998. Roodvoets, Miller & Desjarlais, ORNL and Cool Roof Rating Council, Table 1.
  • 127.6°F with air at 104°F. White TPO sample, Queens Botanical Garden NY, July 2011. Gaffin et al. (2012).
  • After three years of weathering the same membrane read 140.2°F (no air temperature published, so not used to set the bar).

CryoPaint, 85–105°F

  • Rated 0 to 15°F below air temperature on sky-facing surfaces in direct sun, with 94% solar reflectance and 90% 8–13 µm emissivity. The top of the range is the worst case, a few degrees above air. Wind, humidity and tilt beyond 30 degrees reduce the margin.
  • This is a Cryo X Co product specification, not a third-party field measurement, and is the only bar not drawn from published literature.

CryoPaint vs. insulation

Insulation only slows temperature change. CryoPaint actively removes heat, so it follows the air temperature down, about 15°F below it, around the clock.

Air temperature Insulation CryoPaint

CryoPaint

Its surface temperature is a function of the air temperature (air temperature minus 15°F). It does not absorb solar radiation.

Insulation

Its surface temperature is a function of its averaged thermal saturation, usually a mix of ambient convection and direct sun. It only slows or averages temperature change. It does not actively cool or remove heat.

Cooling with physics, not power

Reflects almost all sunlight

94% reflectivity bounces solar energy away before it can heat the surface.

Radiates heat to space

It emits heat through the 8–13 µm atmospheric window, straight out to the cold of deep space.

The same principle keeps spacecraft cool. No moving parts, no power, no emissions. Works on any sky-facing surface: roofs, RVs, containers, enclosures.
